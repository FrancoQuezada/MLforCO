Abstract
Combinatorialoptimization problems present significant computational challenges due to nonconvex feasible regions
and exponentially growing solution spaces. In this paper, we investigate a predict–then–optimize framework that
integratessupervisedMLtechniquesincludingK–NearestNeighbors,DecisionTrees,NaïveBayes,andSupportVector
Machines with mixed integer linear programming to solve combinatorial optimization problems. The framework
is tested on the Multi-item Capacitated Lot-Sizing Problem (MCLSP). Inspired by the Phase I simplex method,
a discrimination rule defines the estimation to ensure a feasible solution. Extensive computational experiments
reveal that using strengthen linear relaxation features to train ML models can reduce resolution times by up to
99% while maintaining optimality gaps below 1%. These results underscore the potential of combining ML and
combinatorial optimization to achieve scalable, near-optimal solutions in time-sensitive decision-making contexts.
Keywords: Predict-then-optimize, Supervised machine learning, Mixed-integer linear programming, Capacitated
lot-sizing problem..
1 Introduction
Combinatorial optimization (CO) problems, especially those formulated as mixed-integer linear program-
ming (MILP models) are inherently challenging due to the nonconvex and discrete nature of their feasible
regions. Unlike linear programming, where feasible solutions can be eﬀiciently explored within a convex set,
MILPs typically require implicit enumeration of feasible discrete solutions, often through branch-and-bound
algorithms (Hoffman, 2000). As a result, solving MILPs becomes computationally expensive, especially for
large-scale instances (Bertsimas and Weismantel, 2005). While exact solutions may be viable at the strate-
gic or tactical level, they are often impractical for operational decision-making, where rapid solutions are
required.
A wide range of techniques have been proposed to address this challenge, including exact methods such
as Benders decomposition (Rahmaniani et al., 2017), Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition (Desaulniers, Desrosiers,
and Solomon, 2006), and cutting-plane methods (Cornuéjols, 2008), which are often combined into branch-
and-cut frameworks. When exact methods are computationally impracticable, heuristics provide a practical
alternative for generating high-quality solutions. These include both classical heuristics based on intuitive
decision rules (Mart, Pardalos, and Resende, 2018) and metaheuristics such as simulated annealing, tabu
search, and ant colony optimization (Desale et al., 2015; Reeves, 1996), which explore the solution space
more systematically. A further evolution involves hyper-heuristics, which dynamically select among a pool
of heuristics based on performance (Burke et al., 2019). More recently, matheuristics, which are hybrid
approaches that embed mathematical programming within metaheuristic frameworks, have been developed

and successfully applied to problems in routing (Archetti and Speranza, 2014), lot-sizing (Cunha, Kramer,
andMelo, 2019), clustering(GnägiandBaumann, 2021), andscheduling(Zhangetal., 2022), amongothers.
Recently, machine learning (ML) models have gained attention as solution methods for combinatorial
optimization models. ML models can be employed either to directly predict solutions or to assist exact
methods, such as guiding the branching decisions in branch-and-bound algorithms. Vanderschueren et al.
(2022) categorize these strategies into two paradigms: predict-then-optimize (sequential) and predict-and-
optimize (integrated). Both approaches aim to reduce the computational effort required to solve large-scale
optimization problems. In particular, the predict-then-optimize strategy offers additional flexibility, as it
enables repeated optimization based on previously solved instances employing imitation learning (Hussein
et al., 2017).

Feasibility is not always ensured. For instance, the approach presented in Lin et al. (2019) applied
decisiontree(DT)andrandomforest(RF)regressionmodelstoapproximateMILPsolutionsforthesecurity
constrained unit commitment (SCUC) problem by first solving the linear relaxation and then predicting the
continuous residual value between the binary and its linear relaxation. They added the predicted residuals
to the relaxed binary value and rounded the result to obtain a candidate integer solution. However, this
prediction could violate SCUC capacity constraints. Similarly, Xavier, Qiu, and Ahmed (2019) utilized
K-nearest neighbor (KNN) and support vector machines (SVM) to enhance MILP solvers, testing their
framework on SCUC problem, by predicting redundant constraints and generating warm starts with KNN,
while SVM is trained to predict aﬀine subspaces where the optimal solution is likely to reside, aiming to
reduce feasible region. However, predicting aﬀine subspaces can lead to infeasible solutions as shown in
computational experiments.
By contrast, works such as Jia and Shen (2019), Morabit, Desaulniers, and Lodi (2023) and Alvarez,
Louveaux,andWehenkel(2014)leveragedmethodslikeBendersdecompositionorcolumngeneration,which
inherentlymaintainfeasibility. Forexample,JiaandShen(2019)trainanSVMclassifiertoidentifyvaluable
Benders cuts to accelerate Benders decomposition for two-stage stochastic programs. Similarly, Morabit,
Desaulniers, and Lodi (2023) proposed an ML approach for arc selection in column generation applied to
constrained shortest path problems. They tested their method on the vehicle and crew scheduling problem
(VCSP)andthevehicleroutingproblemwithtimewindows(VRPTW),bothofwhichinvolveashortestpath
problem with resource constraints as the pricing subproblem. Finally, Alvarez, Louveaux, and Wehenkel
(2014) trained Extremely Randomized Trees to imitate effective branching strategies in the branch-and-
bound algorithm. Additionally, some optimization problems are structured so that infeasible predictions
cannot arise. For example Rachelson, Abbes, and Diemer (2010) integrated MILP with boosting using weak
classifiers (DT and SVM) for intra‐daily electricity planning problem (PP), where unmet demand incurs a
penalty cost rather than violating constraints.

3.2 Imitation learning
In many practical applications, the same optimization problem must be solved repeatedly under varying
input parameters, allowing the collection of a dataset of solved instances. We hypothesize that consistent
patternsintheassignmentofbinarydecisionvariablesacrosstheseinstancescanbemoreeffectivelylearned
through supervised ML techniques than by relying solely on traditional heuristic methods.
One promising method that leverages this idea is imitation learning. According to Hussein et al. (2017),
imitationlearningreferstotheprocessbywhichanagentacquiresskillsorbehaviorsbyobservingateacher
performing a task. In our context, the agent corresponds to the ML model, the teacher is the optimization
solver, and the task is the assignment of binary decision variables in the solution of the problem. The goal
is to train the ML model to replicate the solver’s behavior when faced with new instances.

5 Conclusion
Thispaperproposedamatheuristictosolvecombinatorialoptimization(CO)problemsusingapredict-then-
optimize framework that balances predictive confidence with solution feasibility. The proposed framework
addresses three fundamental challenges in the predict-then-optimize paradigm: feasibility, modelling, and
scalability.
First,regardingfeasibility,traditionalthreshold-basedclassificationdoesnotguaranteefeasiblesolutions
when ML-predicted binary variables are fixed in the MILP. To address this, we proposed a discrimination
rule inspired by Phase I of the Simplex method. This mechanism adjusts infeasible predictions to ensure
feasibilitywhileminimizingthenumberofvariableflips, therebypreservingpredictionquality. Resultsshow
that as prediction accuracy increases, fewer variable flips are required, reinforcing the value of accurate ML
models in CO contexts.
Second, from a modelling perspective, we evaluated the contribution of different feature sets. Features
derived from model parameters (feature set A) and linear relaxations (feature sets B and C) showed that
stronger relaxations significantly enhance ML model accuracy. Additionally, the discrimination rule benefits
from the binary structure of MILPs, enabling eﬀicient feasibility recovery.
Third, the framework demonstrated strong scalability, even on data drift. ML models trained on small
instances(8items)generalizewelltolargerones(32items)whenusingfeaturesets(A)and(C).Featureset
(B), in contrast, requires stronger relaxations to maintain predictive quality at scale.
Finally,experimentalresultsontheMulti-itemCapacitatedLot-SizingProblem(MCLSP)demonstrated
20

--- Página 22 ---
computationaltimereductionsofupto99%comparedtoacommercialsolver(GUROBI),whilemaintaining
optimality gaps consistently below 1% across varying instance sizes and complexities. Thus the proposed
approachisshowntobecompetitiveintermsofsolutionquality,computationtime,andscalabilitycompared
to methods in the existing literature (e.g., (Yilmaz and Büyüktahtakın, 2024)). Unlike the artificial neu-
ral network, it requires significantly fewer solved instances for training and consumes fewer computational
resources, making it attractive for real-world settings where computing infrastructure is limited. This is
especially relevant in operational environments where repetitive, time-sensitive decisions must be made and
deep learning methods may be impractical to implement.
Future research could further investigate the scalability limits of the proposed ML model by evaluating
how effectively models trained on smaller instances generalize to larger problems in terms of item quantity
and planning periods. Additionally, assessing the transferability of this framework to other MILP problems
could highlight its broader applicability and potential limitations. Another promising avenue involves using
predicted scores z, understanding them as probabilities, to guide branching decisions within the search tree,
potentially transforming predictions into guiding for optimal solutions. Moreover, extending the framework
to operate at each node of the branch and bound tree by generating new candidate solutions based on the
linear relaxation at that node could further enhance search eﬀiciency and improve overall solution quality.
Finally, by relying on inherently interpretable techniques, we not only alleviate the “black-box” concerns
of deep learning but also lay the groundwork for future studies to analyze feature importance and uncover
exactly which problem attributes drive high-accuracy predictions. Exploring these dimensions will enhance
the practicality and broader applicability of ML model-integrated optimization methods.
